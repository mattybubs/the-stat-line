Tom Jackson dies at 75 — the Orange Crush linebacker who became the voice of NFL Sundays

By Matt

Tom Jackson, the Broncos' Ring of Fame linebacker and ESPN's first NFL analyst, died Friday at 75. Chris Berman announced the news on Sunday NFL Countdown, and Denver will honor him with a moment of silence before Sunday night's game against the Raiders.

The announcement

Chris Berman opened Sunday NFL Countdown on Sunday morning with news no football fan wanted to hear: his longtime broadcast partner Tom Jackson had died. Jackson passed away Friday at 75, his family confirmed, and the news was unexpected. No cause of death has been revealed.

"All of us are stunned," Berman said on air. "I'm stunned, we're stunned and you're stunned. We've lost one of the best human beings ever to walk the planet, Tom Jackson. I was fortunate to work side by side with Tom for 30 years, primarily on NFL Primetime and Sunday NFL Countdown."

The Denver Broncos confirmed the news in a statement of their own, calling Jackson "a cornerstone of the legendary Orange Crush defense and one of the most beloved players in franchise history." The team will hold a moment of silence and show a tribute video before Sunday night's game against the Raiders. The Countdown crew wore orange ties on Sunday morning in his honor.

Jackson is survived by his wife, Jennifer, and his two daughters, Taylor and Morgan.

The player

Before he was a voice on television, Jackson was one of the fiercest linebackers of his era — and he spent his entire career in one place.

A fourth-round pick out of Louisville in the 1973 draft, Jackson arrived in Denver before the Broncos had ever finished a season with a winning record. He helped change that for good. Over 14 seasons he played 191 games with 177 starts, recording 44 sacks, 20 interceptions and three defensive touchdowns. His seven interceptions in 1976 remain the most by a linebacker in franchise history.

The breakthrough came in 1977, when the Broncos' defense made its name as the "Orange Crush." Jackson was instrumental in the run to the franchise's first playoff berth, AFC title and Super Bowl XII appearance — recovering a fumble and picking off Terry Bradshaw twice in the fourth quarter of Denver's first-ever postseason win, over the Steelers. That season began a three-year stretch in which he made three Pro Bowls and earned two AP All-Pro selections. He finished with two AFC championships, and his teammates voted him their defensive MVP three times and their most inspirational player for six consecutive seasons from 1981 to 1986.

He was also, famously, the loudest man on the field. Early in that 1977 season, after the Broncos upset the defending champion Raiders 30-7, Jackson yelled at Oakland head coach John Madden what became one of the most quoted lines in NFL history: "It's all over, fat man." Madden laughed about it for years afterward. In his 1986 book, Madden named Jackson one of five outside linebackers who could not be ignored, writing that he was "the wildest linebacker I've ever seen. You never knew where he was going to turn up."

Jackson was inducted into the Broncos' Ring of Fame in 1992. He was named to the team's 50th Anniversary Team in 2009 and its all-time Top 100 Team in 2019.

The broadcaster

Jackson's second career was as remarkable as his first — and it started the moment the first one ended.

He joined ESPN in 1987 as the network's first NFL analyst, and for the next three decades he and Berman were the soundtrack of Sunday football. NFL Primetime, with its extended highlight packages, was must-see television in the years before highlights were a tap away, and Jackson's "Jacked Up!" segment — the week's hardest hits — became one of the most anticipated parts of the show.

ESPN chairman Jimmy Pitaro called Jackson "one of the most influential and beloved figures in ESPN's history," and the network's NFL studio carries both men's names: the Chris Berman-Tom Jackson Studio, a distinction unmatched in ESPN history. The Pro Football Hall of Fame honored Jackson with the Pete Rozelle Award for broadcasting excellence in 2015. He retired from ESPN after the 2016 Hall of Fame enshrinement ceremony, then briefly returned in 2019.

As his former teammate Floyd Little once put it: "As good a broadcaster as Tom Jackson is, he was a better football player."

What he meant

Jackson was rare in that he was genuinely great at two completely different jobs, and beloved in both. In Denver he was the emotional leader of a defense that gave the franchise its identity. On television he brought the credibility of an All-Pro and a warmth that made three decades of Sundays feel like a conversation with a friend.

Berman summed him up with a song lyric on Sunday morning: "He's got sunshine on a cloudy day."
