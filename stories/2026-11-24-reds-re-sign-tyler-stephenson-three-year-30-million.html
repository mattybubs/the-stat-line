Reds bring Tyler Stephenson home — three years, $30 million, and the catching hole is closed

By Matt

Cincinnati re-signed its longest-tenured player on Tuesday night, keeping catcher Tyler Stephenson on a three-year deal with an opt-out after year two — the fifth free-agent catcher this decade to command a three-year guarantee.

The move

The Reds finally made their move — and they made it with the player who has been there longer than anyone else on the roster.

Cincinnati and catcher Tyler Stephenson agreed to a three-year, $30 million contract on Tuesday night, ESPN's Jeff Passan first reported, with the deal including an opt-out after the second season and pending a physical. The Cincinnati Enquirer confirmed the agreement through sources, and MLB Trade Rumors, theScore, NBC Sports and Sports Illustrated all corroborated the terms within the hour.

For a team that had looked for nearly a month like it planned to sit out the pre-lockout free agency period entirely, this is a significant strike — exactly one week before the current collective bargaining agreement expires on December 1 and a widely anticipated owners' lockout shuts down baseball business indefinitely.

The player

Stephenson is the longest-tenured player on the Reds' roster, and the only uniform he has ever worn is theirs. Cincinnati drafted him 11th overall in 2015, he debuted in the pandemic-shortened 2020 season, and he has now played 688 big-league games — every one of them as a Red.

The 30-year-old is coming off the best season of his seven-year career by wins above replacement, slashing .241/.323/.404 with 15 home runs, 22 doubles and 54 RBI across 130 games. For his career he is a .257/.335/.422 hitter in just under 2,500 plate appearances.

He is not a perfect catcher — the defense grades out below average and staying healthy has been a recurring battle into his thirties — but the bat plays at a position where offense is scarce, and the Reds know exactly what they are getting.

The summer that made this make sense

This deal only makes sense in the context of July.

Stephenson looked like a trade candidate at the deadline, and he was visibly emotional in a postgame interview when asked about the possibility of being dealt out of the only organization he has ever known. The Reds held him anyway. At the time, the speculation was that they kept him because they believed they could bring him back in free agency — and because they had not held contract extension talks with him in a year and a half, the reunion was anything but certain.

Tuesday night closed that loop. The team that refused to trade him is the team paying him to stay.

The market

Cincinnati did not have much choice but to pay up. The catching market was thin before free agency opened and thinner within days: Ryan Jeffers, considered the top free-agent catcher at the outset, accepted a qualifying offer from Minnesota, leaving Stephenson and the Cubs' Carson Kelly as the top two available backstops.

That scarcity is priced into the deal. Stephenson becomes just the fifth free-agent catcher this decade to command a three-year guarantee — and the only one of that group to also get an opt-out clause. The Reds will roll with a Stephenson–Jose Trevino pairing behind the plate for a third straight season, with top prospect Alfredo Duno — the organization's No. 1 prospect — expected to take over as the everyday catcher in the next few seasons. Stephenson's presence buys Duno time and gives him a veteran to learn from.

What it means

The Reds were one of the least active teams since free agency opened — they could not even keep veteran closer Emilio Pagan, who opted out of the final $10 million year of his deal and quickly signed a one-year, $11.5 million contract with Detroit — and they had just lost reliever Sam Moll to the Cubs hours before the Stephenson news broke.

This is their first big move of the offseason, and it solves their most glaring hole. A team that would have had a gigantic hole at catcher if Stephenson signed elsewhere now has its starter locked up through 2029, with an escape hatch after two years if either side wants one.

For Stephenson, the deal keeps him in the only organization he has ever known — the one that drafted him as a teenager, the one he nearly got traded away from in July, and the one that just made him its catcher of the present while its catcher of the future finishes developing.
