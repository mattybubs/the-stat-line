MLB sues its own players' union before the lockout even starts — the labor fight is now in federal court

By Matt

Six days before the collective bargaining agreement expires, Major League Baseball sued the players' association in federal court — asking a judge to declare the coming lockout legal before the union can challenge it. The union called the suit 'completely frivolous.'

The filing

Major League Baseball took its labor fight to federal court on Wednesday — before the lockout has even begun.

The league sued the Major League Baseball Players Association in the Southern District of New York, in a pre-emptive strike aimed at the union's most powerful legal weapon. The complaint asks a judge to declare that the impending lockout is legal under antitrust law and cannot be enjoined by a court — even if the players give up their union status, as the league anticipates they will.

The suit is a class action, and it names as defendants the eight members of the union's executive subcommittee — including star pitchers Tarik Skubal and Paul Skenes.

The Wall Street Journal first reported the filing. The union answered within hours.

What MLB wants

The league is not seeking damages. It wants a declaratory judgment: a court ruling, in advance, that the lockout is permissible should the union consider disclaiming.

The move is aimed squarely at a maneuver known as a disclaimer of interest. If the players dissolved their union and reconstituted as a trade association, they could argue the lockout is an illegal group boycott and sue MLB on antitrust grounds — the same path NFL players took in 2011, when a court enjoined that league's lockout. The risk for owners is severe: losing an antitrust case could mean treble damages, three times the players' proven losses.

MLB's filing tries to take that option off the table before it is ever used. The league also wants any future litigation kept in the Southern District of New York, where both parties are based, and not "in some unrelated location cherry-picked by the union to litigate their meritless claims."

League spokesman Glen Caplin framed the suit as an attempt to have a judge "proactively disarm the union's nuclear option of disclaiming."

The complaint also alleges the union has failed to negotiate in good faith, and the league said it is imminently filing an unfair labor practice charge with the National Labor Relations Board — arguing the dispute belongs before the NLRB, not a federal court.

The union's answer

The players' association did not wait long to respond, and it did not mince words.

"The lawsuit filed today by MLB is completely frivolous. We will respond appropriately, but MLB's decision to run to court in the middle of negotiations rather than bargain in good faith is incredibly disappointing," the MLBPA said in a statement, reported by The Athletic's Evan Drellich.

The union's position has not moved in months: no salary cap, under any circumstances. Owners have been equally firm — Commissioner Rob Manfred said last week they will not agree to a deal without one.

Why the league moved first

The calendar explains the timing. The current five-year collective bargaining agreement expires Dec. 1 at 11:59 p.m. ET, and owners are expected to lock the players out the moment it does — the sport's 10th work stoppage since 1972, halting signings and trades.

The two sides traded proposals again on Monday, and neither budged on the cap. The owners' standing offer is a $258.2 million ceiling and a $180.2 million payroll floor beginning in 2028, with the current system kept for 2027. The union countered by agreeing to split all local media revenue evenly among the 30 teams — a first for the sport — but without a cap attached.

MLB tweaked its proposal on Tuesday, pledging that all contracts will remain fully guaranteed and no money will be put in escrow if the union accepts a cap and floor. Union head Bruce Meyer immediately condemned it as providing "no real substance."

With talks stalled and the deadline six days away, the league decided to fight the next battle — the courtroom one — before the lockout even starts.

What happens next

The free-agent market has been sprinting to beat the freeze. Skubal got his nine-year, $450 million deal from the Mets done ten days before the shutdown — the richest contract ever for a pitcher, with a $45 million signing bonus paid right away so the lockout cannot touch it. Everyone else in the market has a week to get deals done, or spend the winter wondering.

Negotiations are expected to resume Sunday or Monday in Phoenix, where the union holds its annual executive subcommittee meetings in Scottsdale. The last full lockout, in 2021-22, lasted 99 days and still started the season on time. This one has a salary cap on the table, a federal lawsuit already filed, and a union that has a legal nuclear option of its own.

The fight is no longer just at the bargaining table. It is in court now, too — and opening day is the clock both sides are racing.
