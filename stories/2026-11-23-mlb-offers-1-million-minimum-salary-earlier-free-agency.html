MLB offers a $1 million minimum salary and earlier free agency — but only if players accept a salary cap

By Matt

With eight days left before the collective bargaining agreement expires, MLB offered a $1 million minimum salary, earlier free agency and a bigger pre-arbitration pool on Monday — all conditioned on a salary cap the union has vowed never to accept.

The offer

Major League Baseball put its most player-friendly proposal of the labor fight on the table Monday — and tied every dollar of it to a salary cap.

The league agreed to raise the minimum salary to $1 million for all players beginning next season, up from $780,000. It would be the largest minimum-salary increase in baseball history, eclipsing the previous record jump of $129,500.

Free agency would come sooner, too. MLB formally accepted the union's proposal to cut the service-time requirement from six years to five for players who turn 30, with the team keeping the right to sign the player to the average salary of the 125 highest-paid players. According to MLB's research, the change would affect 512 players on 40-man rosters.

The pre-arbitration bonus pool would grow 30 percent, from $50 million to $65 million, rising to $75 million before the end of the next collective bargaining agreement. Players who spent at least one full season in the major leagues would receive at least $1.1 million through the pre-arbitration bonus program. MLB claims 57 percent of all players on 40-man rosters would benefit from the increase beginning next season.

Two more sweeteners came with it: an automatic 5 percent signing bonus for arbitration-eligible players who sign without going through the arbitration process, and a yearly, centrally funded $500,000 bonus for one veteran per team with at least six years of major-league service who signs a one-year contract for $10 million or less. MLB also proposed eliminating the qualifying offer and draft-pick compensation in free agency.

The catch

None of it happens unless the players accept a salary cap and a payroll floor.

Commissioner Rob Manfred has said owners will agree to a deal only if it includes one, and the league's standing proposal sets the cap at $258.2 million and the floor at $180.2 million, starting in 2028. The union has vowed never to accept a cap.

MLB spokesman Glen Caplin framed the offer as meeting the players' objectives: "Every other major U.S. sport has a salary cap and floor system to limit payroll disparity, and the fact remains that growth in player compensation has been slower in baseball than every one of those leagues. MLB is trying to reach an agreement with the players by meeting their objectives on a number of important issues, including earlier access to free agency and increased compensation for players earlier in their careers."

The union's interim executive director, Bruce Meyer, did not buy it: "The league's proposals today don't add a single dollar in overall player compensation as compared to prior proposals, and once again are expressly conditioned on accepting their salary cap and other anti-player restrictions. Any supposed benefits to certain classes of players are simply taken out of the pockets of other players."

The union's counter

The players answered Monday with a proposal of their own: share all media revenue equally among the 30 clubs — without a cap attached.

That is a real concession in one direction. The union formally agreed to the owners' proposal to equally share all MLB media revenues, a first for the sport. Local media revenue has fallen to $1.8 billion this year, down $575 million from a high of about $2.4 billion annually from 2021 to 2024, as cable networks have declined.

The union is also proposing that low-revenue teams receive more than $950 million beginning in 2027, up from about $700 million this past season, with rewards designed to discourage tanking: $20 million for revenue-sharing recipients that qualify for the postseason or finish with a better record than the worst postseason qualifier, and $10 million for finishing above .500.

What happens next

The clock is the story now. The current five-year labor contract expires Dec. 1 at 11:59 p.m. EST, and management is expected to lock out the players — the sport's 10th work stoppage since 1972, bringing signings and trades to a halt.

The two sides are expected to resume negotiations Tuesday in New York, take a break through the Thanksgiving holidays, and perhaps pick up again during the weekend in Phoenix, when the union holds its annual meeting with its executive sub-committee.

The talks resumed one day after Tarik Skubal agreed to a $450 million, nine-year contract with the Mets, the richest pitcher deal in baseball history — and the market has been sprinting ever since, with Paul Goldschmidt signing with the Mets on Monday as well. Everyone in the game knows what Dec. 1 means. The question is whether eight days is enough to bridge a gap that has not moved in months.
