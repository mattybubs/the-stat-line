Nvidia just brought Jensen Huang back — 11 days after he resigned, and six days after the board said it never would

By Matt

Nvidia's board voted unanimously on Monday to reinstate Jensen Huang as president and CEO, 11 days after he resigned over extramarital affairs — and six days after the board's chair said it did not intend to bring him back. Shares surged more than 31% in premarket trading.

Eleven days. That is how long Nvidia went without Jensen Huang — and how long it took the most valuable company in the world to decide it could not live without him.

Nvidia announced on Monday morning that Huang has been reinstated as president and chief executive officer and as a member of the board, effective immediately. The board's vote was unanimous. The announcement, released at about 8:55 a.m. ET, ends the strangest 11 days in the company's history — a span that started with a resignation, ran through a stock rout, a public campaign for his return, and a board chair saying on an earnings call that the company did not intend to bring him back.

The reversal

The whiplash is the story. On November 12, Huang resigned after revelations of at least two extramarital affairs — one with a businesswoman whom United States authorities suspect of spying for China, an allegation Beijing denies, and another with an Nvidia employee. The board accepted the resignation, named chief financial officer Colette Kress interim CEO, and appointed longtime director Harvey C. Jones as its first-ever board chair. There was no succession plan in place, and the board had no chair before that day.

Five days later, on the company's earnings call, Jones closed the door on a return. The board had "unanimously accepted" Huang's resignation, he said, and "does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO." The board retained the search firm Heidrick & Struggles to find a permanent replacement.

Six days after that, the same board voted unanimously to bring him back.

"The Board believes Jensen is the right person to lead NVIDIA, and that his reinstatement is in the best interests of our stockholders, employees, partners and customers," Jones said in Monday's announcement. The board said it reached the decision after "a series of discussions and meetings" with Huang, and that it was satisfied with his candor and his ability to lead.

Why the board changed its mind

Two forces pushed the board off its position: the market and the White House.

Nvidia shed more than $600 billion in market value in the week after Huang's resignation, and the selloff spilled across the AI trade. A petition calling for his reinstatement drew more than 180,000 signatures, including one from Elon Musk. President Donald Trump said last week that he wanted to see Huang back in the top job, welcomed the decision in a Truth Social post on Monday, and told CNBC he called the board and helped orchestrate the return.

The board also addressed the question that made this more than a personal scandal. In reaching its decision, it said it received input from senior levels of the United States government, and based on that input, it is confident Huang's reinstatement poses no national security risk. That matters because the affair at the center of the controversy raised questions about Nvidia's China business and the export of advanced AI chips — a debate that has been running in Washington for years.

Huang, for his part, sounded like a man who knows how close he came to losing everything. "I am honored to return to NVIDIA and lead one of the most consequential technology companies in the world at the beginning of a new industrial revolution," he said in the announcement. "I take this responsibility with the greatest humility and renewed commitment."

The market's answer

Investors did not wait to render a verdict. Nvidia shares surged more than 31% in premarket trading and leapt 33% once the market opened, while the Nasdaq rallied 6% and the S&P 500 rose 4% — making up for Friday's plunge, when investors had dumped AI-exposed shares in part because of the uncertainty at Nvidia itself.

It is a remarkable swing for a company whose founder built it from a video-game chip designer into the dominant supplier of the processors that run the AI boom. Huang co-founded Nvidia in 1993 and has led it for 33 years — which is why the board's problem was never really about finding a replacement. It was about whether the company that grew up around one man could function without him. Eleven days was the answer.

What is not over

The reinstatement does not close the book. The board said an independent committee of directors will continue investigating reports that Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort, and that the investigation "will proceed to conclusion." Huang fully supports the investigation and is committed to cooperating with it, the company said.

The board has also formed a new National Security and Export Control Committee, made up solely of independent directors, to oversee trade compliance and national security risks, and it is hiring a chief compliance officer — the kind of guardrails a board builds when it has just learned what happens without them.

Kress returns to her role as chief financial officer after 11 days as interim CEO. Jones remains board chair. And Huang — who apologized after his resignation and said he would return if invited — is back in the job he held for more than three decades, at a company that just proved, in public, that it could not replace him.
