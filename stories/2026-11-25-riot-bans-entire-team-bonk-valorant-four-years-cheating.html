Riot banned an entire Valorant team for four years — every player and the coach, for cheating in a qualifier

By Matt

Riot Games has banned all five players of Team BONK and their head coach from all Riot-sanctioned competition after its Anti-Cheat team confirmed unauthorized software was running during the VCT EMEA Kickoff Open Qualifier — and Riot says every player knowingly took part.

Riot Games has handed down one of the harshest punishments in Valorant history. All five players of Team BONK and their head coach have been banned from competition for 48 months after Riot's Anti-Cheat team confirmed unauthorized software was running during official matches in the VCT EMEA Kickoff Open Qualifier. The ban is effective immediately, covers every Riot-sanctioned esports competition, and applies equally to the coach who ran the software and the five players Riot says knowingly took part in the scheme.

The ruling, published by Riot's Competitive Operations team on Tuesday, closes a case that started with an upset nobody expected. BONK played in the VCT EMEA Kickoff Open Qualifier #1 on November 6 and 7, and stunned Turkish organization Aurora Gaming — a result that immediately drew suspicion. Reports of possible cheating prompted Riot's Anti-Cheat team to investigate, and the findings were damning: unauthorized software was actively running on a computer linked to BONK's head coach, Massimo#365, during official matches, feeding him live information about the opposing players' positions.

How the case unfolded

Riot moved fast once the investigation confirmed what happened. BONK was disqualified from the qualifier on November 8, and the affected matches were replayed. Formal disciplinary proceedings opened on November 10, with each of the six individuals given a chance to respond before the ruling landed. The ruling does not name the specific software that was used, or detail how the information was shared with the players — but it is explicit that the arrangement was deliberate, and that the deliberate nature of the misconduct shaped the length of the punishment.

The six banned individuals are head coach Massimo#365 and players WaddlesThePig, kubfu, Yaquob "Corsa" Hanif, Aleks "Kaaru" Grudniewski, and Max "deqadz" Vinall. All six were found to have breached Article 4.3 of Riot's Esports Global Code of Conduct, which prohibits cheating with unauthorized methods or technology — and also prohibits knowingly participating in, enabling, or assisting cheating. That second half is why the players received the same punishment as the coach. Riot did not need to prove that each player had cheating software installed on their own computer to find them in breach.

Why the ban reaches so far

The suspensions are separate from the disqualification, and their scope is what makes this a career-altering ruling. Because the bans cover all Riot-sanctioned esports competitions, the six cannot regain eligibility by joining another Valorant organization or switching to a different Riot title. With the suspensions taking effect in November 2026, they are out of official competition until late 2030 — effectively the rest of the VCT 2027 season and three more after it.

The other side of the ruling is Aurora Gaming, the team BONK beat to trigger the investigation. Aurora was reinstated after the disqualification, with the affected matches replayed, and qualified for the EMEA Qualifier playoffs starting November 25. The team that lost to a cheater gets its shot; the team that cheated loses four years.

What this says about the VCT's open qualifiers

The case is a warning shot at the exact point where the VCT is most vulnerable. Open qualifiers are the on-ramp for unknown teams — anyone can enter, and a deep run can launch a career. That openness is also the risk: a team nobody has heard of beating an established organization is exactly the kind of result that invites suspicion, and here the suspicion was right. Riot's answer is a punishment with no ambiguity in it: cheat in a qualifier and the whole roster goes down, coach included, for four years.

It also shows how much Riot is willing to invest in policing the lower rungs of its own system. This was not a Champions final or a Masters event — it was an open qualifier in early November, and Riot still ran a full Anti-Cheat investigation, a formal disciplinary process, and a public ruling. For every other team grinding through the open bracket this season, the message is now written down in the rulebook and in the record: knowing about it counts the same as doing it.
