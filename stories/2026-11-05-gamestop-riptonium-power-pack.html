GameStop's $10,000 Pokémon pack gives you a 44.8% chance of pulling a card worth $6,000–$8,000

By Matt

GameStop's new Riptonium Power Pack costs $10,000 for one PSA-graded Pokémon card — and GameStop's own odds say nearly 75% of pulls come back worth less than the pack. The top chase is a $156,000 Lugia.

Disclosure: this post contains affiliate links. I earn a commission on qualifying purchases.

What happened

GameStop has gone all in on the digital pack. The retailer announced Riptonium on October 10 — its biggest Power Pack tier yet — and the price is exactly what it sounds like: $10,000 for a single digital pack that reveals one PSA-graded Pokémon card.

The catch is right there on GameStop's own listing. According to the odds GameStop publishes for the pack, 44.8% of pulls land in the $6,000–$8,000 estimated value band. That is at least $2,000 less than the pack costs, before resale fees or the gap between an estimate and what a buyer can actually get for the card.

The odds, straight from GameStop

Here is the full value chart for Riptonium, as reported from GameStop's listing:

$6,000–$8,000: 44.8%

$8,000–$10,000: 29.9%

$10,000–$20,000: 22.9%

$20,000–$40,000: 1.5%

$40,000–$80,000: 0.5%

Above $80,000: 0.4%

Add it up and 74.7% of pulls come back worth less than the $10,000 pack price. Only 2.4% of pulls land above $20,000. The top pull currently listed is valued at $156,000 — a PSA 10 2005 #105 Lugia ex-Holo, per Resell Calendar's report on the pool.

GameStop warns on the listing that values can fluctuate and that the probabilities update every five minutes. The estimates are calculated from market data and feed GameStop's instant buyback offers, which pay 90% of the estimated value. So even a "break-even" pull at $10,000 is worth $9,000 the moment you take the buyback.

How Power Packs work

Riptonium is the top rung of a ladder GameStop has been building all year. Power Packs are digital mystery packs made in partnership with PSA: you buy a pack online, it opens digitally to reveal a real, physical, PSA-graded card, and that card goes into your PSA Vault — a secure, insured storage facility in Delaware — until you decide to ship it, sell it, or keep it stored. Every card was already graded and slabbed before you spent a dollar.

The platform launched to the public on April 15, 2026 with four categories — Pokémon, football, basketball, and baseball — and nine price tiers: Starter ($25), Silver ($50), Gold ($100), Sapphire ($250), Platinum ($500), Diamond ($1,000), Lunar ($2,500), Neutronium ($5,000), and Riptonium ($10,000). Higher tiers buy a deeper card pool and a higher ceiling. The category list has since grown to include One Piece and themed Pokémon packs like Rewind and On Fire.

GameStop added the $5,000 Neutronium tier after announcing its bid for eBay, and Riptonium now pushes the ceiling to $10,000. It is, by a wide margin, the most expensive single digital pack in the hobby.

Why people are buying it anyway

The math says most buyers lose money. The hobby says that was never the point.

Power Packs sell the rip — the moment of the reveal, live on a creator's stream, with a real slab on the line. GameStop publishes live pull data, so collectors can watch in real time what other people are hitting. And for the 0.4%, there is a $156,000 Lugia sitting at the top of the pool.

That is the same logic that has powered the hobby's biggest nights all year, from Logan Paul's $1.2 million vintage rip to the record $16.49 million Pikachu Illustrator sale. The difference is that GameStop has industrialized it: nine tiers, published odds, a vault, and a buyback button. The casino is open, the odds are printed on the door, and the line is still forming.
