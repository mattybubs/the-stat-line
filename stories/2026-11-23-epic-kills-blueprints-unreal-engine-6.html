Epic is killing Blueprints in Unreal Engine 6 — and betting AI can port your game for you

By Matt

Unreal Engine 6 will ship without Blueprints or the Actor Gameplay Framework — a reversal of Epic's June plan. Gameplay moves to Verse and Scene Graph, and Epic's pitch is that AI will convert your UE5 project for you.

Blueprints are the reason a huge share of modern games exist at all. The visual scripting system — wiring boxes together instead of writing code — has been part of Unreal Engine since UE4, and it turned level designers and artists into people who could build gameplay themselves. Now Epic is taking it away.

Unreal Engine 6 will not include Blueprints or the Actor Gameplay Framework. Not in the full release, and not in Early Access either. Gameplay in UE6 gets built in Verse, Epic's own programming language, on top of a new framework called Scene Graph. That is the announcement Epic's Marcus Wassmer, the company's executive vice president of development, made in a post on the Unreal Engine blog on November 20 — and the industry spent today arguing about it.

What changed

This is a reversal, and a fast one. In June, at the State of Unreal, Epic laid out a gentler transition: Blueprints and Actors would live alongside the new systems in UE6 Early Access and 6.0, so existing UE5 projects could move across one piece at a time while Scene Graph matured. They would be deprecated eventually, once the new framework was ready.

Five months later, that plan is gone. Epic CEO Tim Sweeney announced the harder break at Unreal Fest Tokyo on November 3 and 4, and apologized for it on stage, calling it a painful change. Wassmer's post on November 20 made it official and put the reasoning in writing.

The C++ gameplay framework is going too. C++ stays for core engine systems like rendering and physics, but game logic in UE6 is Verse and Scene Graph, full stop. UE6 Early Access is still targeted for the end of 2027.

Why Epic says it's doing this

The reason, in one word, is AI. Blueprints are stored as binary files, and large language models cannot read them directly. Code, they can. Wassmer's post is blunt about the bet: "with recent advances in AI capability, we expect AI to massively help with converting UE5 projects to UE6, if not make that process fully automatic."

Epic's argument has two legs. The first is the cost curve: citing Epoch AI research, the post says the cost of any given AI task has fallen 13 times year over year, and Epic expects that trend to continue — so by the time UE6 ships, converting a game should be cheap. The second is a proof of concept at enormous scale. Fortnite has one of the largest Blueprint libraries anywhere, and Epic says it used AI to convert all of the Battle Royale Blueprints to Verse in about two weeks — a job the company expected to take years. The result is a working prototype of Fortnite running on roughly 3 million lines of Verse, and the team is still validating its performance, memory use, and architecture.

That caveat matters. A prototype that boots is not a shipped game, and Epic is asking every studio on UE5 to trust that the same pipeline will hold up on code it has never seen. But the scale of the Fortnite test is also the reason this announcement landed the way it did: it is not a promise about a future tool, it is a claim about something Epic says it already did.

Who this hits hardest

The studios that leaned on Blueprints the most are the ones with the most to lose. Sandfall Interactive has said its award-winning Clair Obscur: Expedition 33 was 95 percent built with Blueprints — one of the year's most celebrated games, made largely without writing code. That was supposed to be the proof the system worked. Instead, Epic is removing it.

The reaction from developers has been swift and skeptical. Former Valve writer Chet Faliszek said on social media that the change complicates cross-discipline work — the whole point of Blueprints was that a designer could take charge of a piece of gameplay without handing it to a programmer — and that his team will be staying on Unreal Engine 5. The prompt-based model, in his telling, takes that control back out of designers' hands.

Epic knows this is a lot to ask. Wassmer's post closes by thanking developers "for bearing with us while we chart the course to a new, modern engine," and the company is not forcing anyone off UE5 tomorrow. It says it will keep developing targeted UE5 features and providing fixes and support while UE6 is built, and it has already started bringing UE6's AI tooling back to UE5 — expanded Python scripting, Model Context Protocol improvements, web as a target platform, and a collaboration feature called Unreal Canvas all arrived in the UE 5.9 preview on November 9.

The bigger picture

The Blueprints decision does not exist in a vacuum. It follows Epic's broader shift toward an AI-first business, and it comes as the company argues openly that the engine has to change because AI coding tools are getting good enough to build games without one. Epic's chief operating officer Daniel Vogel told IEEE Spectrum the company is "pivoting hard towards velocity," because a static engine's features can eventually be replicated by a model. Unreal is the second most commonly used engine after Unity, and first by the number of games sold — Epic is betting that position on the idea that the future of making games is describing what you want in code, not wiring boxes on a screen.

Whether that bet pays off is now the argument of the year in game development. The June plan gave studios a bridge. The November plan gives them a deadline and a promise that AI will build the bridge for them. UE6 Early Access, targeted for the end of 2027, is when everyone finds out which one of those was real.
