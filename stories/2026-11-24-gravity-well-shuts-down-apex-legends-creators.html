Apex Legends veterans' studio Gravity Well is shutting down — without ever releasing a game

By Matt

Gravity Well, the anti-crunch studio founded by Apex Legends veterans Drew McCoy and Jon Shiring, is going into 'hibernation' after losing its funding in the spring — six years in, its debut game never saw the light of day.

Gravity Well is done. The studio founded in 2020 by former Apex Legends executive producer Drew McCoy and lead software engineer Jon Shiring updated its website on November 24 to say it is going into "hibernation" — the polite word for a studio that has run out of money and out of road. Six years after it opened its doors, Gravity Well is closing without ever releasing a game.

The statement, signed by McCoy, Shiring, and chief operating officer Mon Saetern, is blunt about what happened. "In the Spring of 2026 we lost our funding support, and like so many other studios this year, we were unable to secure replacement funds to keep the team on to finish our game." The studio added that it still believes in the project it was building, and called the team it assembled "truly world class" — then turned the statement into a job reference, telling hiring managers that any Gravity Well resume that lands on their desk "comes with our highest recommendation." Publishers and investors interested in the game are invited to get in touch.

The warning signs were there in October

The hibernation announcement did not come out of nowhere. On October 6, Gravity Well confirmed it had laid off more than 40 people — the majority of its team — after losing its funding over the summer. The studio described the cuts as a "fight for survival" and admitted at the time that "given the current state of the industry our future is uncertain." It kept going with a skeleton crew while it searched for a new backer. That search has now failed.

What the team was actually making remains a mystery. Gravity Well never formally unveiled its debut title, though reporting at the time of the layoffs described it as a brand-new IP believed to be a third-person shooter. The studio released just two pieces of concept art for the game — one of them the coastal town and seaplane image above — and that is effectively all the public ever saw of it.

A studio built on a promise the industry couldn't keep

Gravity Well launched with a pitch that stood out at the time: a remote-native studio where team health was the top priority, crunch was off the table, pay was good, and every developer had real creative freedom. McCoy said at the time that the point was to "shake things up in AAA game dev." The founders brought serious pedigree — both had worked on Call of Duty, Titanfall, and Apex Legends at Respawn — and the studio planned to stay deliberately small, peaking at around 80 people.

It was a bet on a funding climate that no longer exists. The studio was one of many formed during the pandemic-era investment boom, when money flowed freely into new teams. As that money dried up, those studios have been falling one by one — Gravity Well is only the latest, and its story is the bleakest version of the pattern: not a game that flopped, not a studio bought and gutted, but a team that never got to show anyone what it made.

The door is technically still open. "Hibernation" is not "closed," and the statement leaves room for a publisher or investor to revive the project. But with the team scattered and the funding gone, what Gravity Well built now exists only as two pieces of concept art and a recommendation letter.
