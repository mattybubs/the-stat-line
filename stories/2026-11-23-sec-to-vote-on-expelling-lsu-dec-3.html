The SEC will vote December 3 on whether to kick LSU out of the conference

By Matt

The SEC filed an amended federal complaint Monday and set a December 3 vote of league presidents on terminating LSU's membership — the second time this fall the league has put expulsion on the table, and a step the conference has never taken against a member.

The vote

The Southeastern Conference will vote on whether to kick LSU out of the league.

The SEC announced Monday that it filed an amended complaint and a motion for a temporary restraining order in its federal lawsuit against Louisiana State University, and that the conference's presidents and chancellors will meet Thursday, December 3, to deliberate LSU's ongoing membership in the conference. The court filing goes further than the public statement: the league's CEOs, it says, intend to hold the December 3 special meeting and "consider termination of LSU's membership in the SEC."

Commissioner Greg Sankey made clear in a signed affidavit that the vote is happening regardless of what LSU does next. Termination of LSU's membership, he wrote, "will be considered whether or not LSU continues to exclude Mr. Luis from participating in basketball games as a player on the LSU team."

That is the sentence that turns a legal fight into an existential one. LSU has already done what the league asked on the player at the center of it. The SEC is voting anyway.

How we got here

The flashpoint is men's basketball guard RJ Luis Jr., whom the SEC deemed ineligible. LSU played him anyway last week against Louisiana-Monroe, after a Louisiana court barred the conference from penalizing the school for rostering him. LSU president Wade Rousse then instructed head coach Will Wade not to play Luis, and Luis has since been removed from the roster.

But the Luis case is the second act, not the first. The feud started in September, when LSU football — under first-year head coach Lane Kiffin — tried to add two former NFL players, tight end Dae'Quan Wright and defensive lineman Zxavian Harris, to its roster. The SEC threatened expulsion then, too, and convened the other conference schools on September 10 to discuss it. No vote was taken, LSU backed down and declined to roster either player, and the league said discussions would continue toward an "appropriate resolution."

They continued, all right. The SEC's amended complaint now accuses LSU of conduct that confirms "the lack of institutional control over the athletics program at LSU that is required by Article 6 of the SEC Constitution." The league's legal theory, laid out in its filing, is a First Amendment freedom-of-association argument: like a private association, it says it has the right to expel a member whose conduct contradicts its message. The SEC is also asking a federal judge in Alabama to keep anyone from going to court to block the December 3 meeting.

The bar — and the history

Removing LSU would take a two-thirds vote of the conference's members, with LSU presumably not voting to expel itself.

It would also be something the SEC has never done. No member has ever been voted out of the conference. The three schools that left — Sewanee in 1940, Georgia Tech in 1964, Tulane in 1966 — all walked away voluntarily. LSU is a founding member, and one of the league's most valuable and successful on-field brands.

That is why the threat lands the way it does even if the votes are not there. When the idea first surfaced in September, Senator John Kennedy of Louisiana called it "bone deep down to the marrow stupid." The skepticism has not gone away — but the vote is now scheduled, with a date, after the league went to federal court to protect its right to hold it.

What's at stake

Expulsion would end LSU's membership in every sport, not just the one that started the fight. Football, basketball, baseball, all of it — out of the conference LSU helped build, with no obvious landing spot that replaces the money, the schedule, or the recruiting footprint of the SEC.

And the stakes run past Baton Rouge. The SEC is suing one of its own members in federal court to establish that it can enforce its eligibility rules after years of court rulings that have cut the other way. If the league wins that argument, every conference in the country gains a precedent. If it loses — or if its own presidents blink on December 3 — the message is that a member can defy the league in court and survive.

What's next

The December 3 meeting will be held by videoconference. The federal court still has to rule on the SEC's request for a temporary restraining order, and no hearing date has been made public. Between now and then, expect Louisiana politicians, LSU leadership, and the other 15 SEC schools to spend ten days counting votes in public and in private.

The vote itself is the story. Whatever the presidents decide on December 3, a conference putting a founding member's existence to a vote is the kind of thing that only happens once — and college sports will be living with the answer for a long time.
