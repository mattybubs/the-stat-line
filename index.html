LIV Golf is shutting down after five years and $5 billion

By Matt

LIV Golf told a bankruptcy court Thursday it is winding down operations and liquidating its assets — no LIV 2.0, no 2027 season — after its $300 million rescue fell apart.

Disclosure: this post contains affiliate links. I earn a commission on qualifying purchases.

LIV Golf is dead

LIV Golf is shutting down. The league told a U.S. bankruptcy court in Trenton, New Jersey, on Thursday, November 5, that it is abandoning its plans for a restructured LIV 2.0 and will instead wind down operations and liquidate its assets. The wind-down begins Monday, November 9.

"The debtors are not currently proceeding with the LIV 2.0 plan," attorney Matthew Williams said in court. "Instead, the debtors are toggling to a value-maximizing wind-down in their business."

The hearing lasted 13 minutes. Five years after it launched to upend professional golf, LIV is done.

How it fell apart

The collapse came fast once the money went. Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund, which bankrolled LIV from its June 2022 launch and poured more than $5 billion into the venture, announced in April 2026 that it would end funding after the 2026 season. LIV filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in New Jersey on September 8, insisting the move was meant to clear the way for a player-owned LIV 2.0 for 2027.

For a moment it looked like a rescue was coming. On October 5, LIV announced a deal with BC Partners Credit worth up to $300 million, including a $4 million initial investment. But the deal depended on enough players committing to the new version — and the commitments never came. On Wednesday, BC Partners pulled out, and by Thursday morning the league's lawyers were in court confirming the wind-down.

"We believe the wind down path is the best path," Williams told the court. In a press release, LIV's board committee said "shifting toward an orderly wind-down is the most responsible path forward for the League at this time."

LIV's attorneys intend to file a plan for winding down operations by November 9. The creditors committee meets November 20, with another bankruptcy hearing set for November 24. The league says it will remain open to third-party offers that could extract more value from its assets during the wind-down — but barring a last-minute investor, LIV will not exist beyond this year.

What it was

LIV launched in June 2022 with one pitch: Saudi money, nine-figure signing bonuses, and a 54-hole, team-based format that broke nearly every convention of professional golf. It lured some of the biggest names in the sport away from the PGA Tour — Dustin Johnson, Jon Rahm, Sergio Garcia, Brooks Koepka, Bryson DeChambeau — and fractured the sport for four years. The PGA Tour and LIV spent years in courtrooms and boardrooms, at one point announcing a framework agreement that never became a deal.

The money was staggering and the burn rate was worse. More than $5 billion from the PIF, and by April the fund decided the circuit no longer fit its investment strategy. CEO Scott O'Neil said as recently as August 5 that LIV had secured funding to keep the league alive. Two months later it was in bankruptcy court.

The human cost is already visible. Jon Rahm, the league's biggest signing, agreed to forfeit all claims against LIV — said to be worth some $100 million — to get out of his contract. Rahm, Garcia, Ian Poulter, and Tyrrell Hatton are among the notable players who have left LIV this year. Around 30 golfers had committed to the planned LIV 2.0, with another 10 undecided — not enough to save it. Players who left for LIV face a one-year suspension on the PGA Tour from the date of their last LIV tournament, making Rahm and others eligible beginning August 24, 2027.

Even in collapse, the numbers tell the story of how the money moved: LIV paid $15.6 million to retain seven of its executives in the months between the PIF pulling funding and the bankruptcy filing, including $6.375 million to CEO Scott O'Neil.

What happens now

• Monday, November 9: the wind-down and liquidation process officially begins.

• November 20: LIV's creditors committee meets.

• November 24: another hearing in the bankruptcy case.

• The players: stars who left are already scattering — Rahm and others are eligible to return to the PGA Tour in August 2027 after serving their one-year suspensions.

• The assets: LIV's remaining assets will be sold off to maximize value for creditors.

Five years, $5 billion, and a sport split in two. LIV Golf is shutting down.

GameStop's $10,000 Pokémon pack gives you a 44.8% chance of pulling a card worth $6,000–$8,000

By Matt

GameStop's new Riptonium Power Pack costs $10,000 for one PSA-graded Pokémon card — and GameStop's own odds say nearly 75% of pulls come back worth less than the pack. The top chase is a $156,000 Lugia.

Disclosure: this post contains affiliate links. I earn a commission on qualifying purchases.

What happened

GameStop has gone all in on the digital pack. The retailer announced Riptonium on October 10 — its biggest Power Pack tier yet — and the price is exactly what it sounds like: $10,000 for a single digital pack that reveals one PSA-graded Pokémon card.

The catch is right there on GameStop's own listing. According to the odds GameStop publishes for the pack, 44.8% of pulls land in the $6,000–$8,000 estimated value band. That is at least $2,000 less than the pack costs, before resale fees or the gap between an estimate and what a buyer can actually get for the card.

The odds, straight from GameStop

Here is the full value chart for Riptonium, as reported from GameStop's listing:

$6,000–$8,000: 44.8%

$8,000–$10,000: 29.9%

$10,000–$20,000: 22.9%

$20,000–$40,000: 1.5%

$40,000–$80,000: 0.5%

Above $80,000: 0.4%

Add it up and 74.7% of pulls come back worth less than the $10,000 pack price. Only 2.4% of pulls land above $20,000. The top pull currently listed is valued at $156,000 — a PSA 10 2005 #105 Lugia ex-Holo, per Resell Calendar's report on the pool.

GameStop warns on the listing that values can fluctuate and that the probabilities update every five minutes. The estimates are calculated from market data and feed GameStop's instant buyback offers, which pay 90% of the estimated value. So even a "break-even" pull at $10,000 is worth $9,000 the moment you take the buyback.

How Power Packs work

Riptonium is the top rung of a ladder GameStop has been building all year. Power Packs are digital mystery packs made in partnership with PSA: you buy a pack online, it opens digitally to reveal a real, physical, PSA-graded card, and that card goes into your PSA Vault — a secure, insured storage facility in Delaware — until you decide to ship it, sell it, or keep it stored. Every card was already graded and slabbed before you spent a dollar.

The platform launched to the public on April 15, 2026 with four categories — Pokémon, football, basketball, and baseball — and nine price tiers: Starter ($25), Silver ($50), Gold ($100), Sapphire ($250), Platinum ($500), Diamond ($1,000), Lunar ($2,500), Neutronium ($5,000), and Riptonium ($10,000). Higher tiers buy a deeper card pool and a higher ceiling. The category list has since grown to include One Piece and themed Pokémon packs like Rewind and On Fire.

GameStop added the $5,000 Neutronium tier after announcing its bid for eBay, and Riptonium now pushes the ceiling to $10,000. It is, by a wide margin, the most expensive single digital pack in the hobby.

Why people are buying it anyway

The math says most buyers lose money. The hobby says that was never the point.

Power Packs sell the rip — the moment of the reveal, live on a creator's stream, with a real slab on the line. GameStop publishes live pull data, so collectors can watch in real time what other people are hitting. And for the 0.4%, there is a $156,000 Lugia sitting at the top of the pool.

That is the same logic that has powered the hobby's biggest nights all year, from Logan Paul's $1.2 million vintage rip to the record $16.49 million Pikachu Illustrator sale. The difference is that GameStop has industrialized it: nine tiers, published odds, a vault, and a buyback button. The casino is open, the odds are printed on the door, and the line is still forming.

Wembanyama's 52 points, 24 rebounds lift Spurs past Mavericks

By Matt

Victor Wembanyama dropped a career-high 52 points with 24 rebounds and 8 blocks, sinking the deciding free throw with 24.2 seconds left as the Spurs edged the Mavericks 112-111 for their sixth straight win.

Disclosure: this post contains affiliate links. I earn a commission on qualifying purchases.

What happened

The San Antonio Spurs edged the Dallas Mavericks 112-111 on November 4, and the entire night belonged to Victor Wembanyama.

Wembanyama finished with 52 points, 24 rebounds, and 8 blocks — a line that reads like a misprint and still somehow undersells how much of the game ran through him.

The deciding moment

With 24.2 seconds left and the game hanging in the balance, Wembanyama stepped to the line and sank the deciding free throw to put San Antonio back on top. One point was all the margin the Spurs needed.

A historic half

Wembanyama did not wait until the fourth quarter to make history. He became the first player in 30 seasons to record at least 25 points, 10 rebounds, and five blocks in a single half.

That is the kind of first-half line that usually takes a player a full game to reach. Wembanyama did it in one half, against a division rival, in a game his team went on to win by a single point.

Why this one is historic

The full line is where it gets absurd. Wembanyama is the first player in 52 years to put up at least 50 points, 20 rebounds, and 5 blocks in a game. The last man to do it was Bob McAdoo in 1974 — 52 points, 22 rebounds, 7 blocks.

Since the NBA started officially tracking blocks in the 1973-74 season, exactly two players have ever posted a 50-20-5 game: McAdoo and Wembanyama. Wilt Chamberlain did the 50-20 part at least five times, but all of those came before blocks were an official stat, so his lines can't be measured the same way. Wembanyama's 8 blocks are also the most by any player in a 50-point game since tracking began, and his 24 rebounds are the most in a 50-point game since Chris Webber went for 51 and 26 in 2001. He is the first Spur in franchise history with a 50-20 game.

And he did it while sick. Wembanyama said after the game that he had been battling a virus — "I haven't been feeling great lately. I've been sick for a while. And today, I felt like I got my legs back, so it should be more like this more often." He had scored just 12 in the previous game against Utah, then rolled his right ankle with 9:11 left in the fourth against Dallas, briefly left the game, and still came back to close it out. His own read on the night: he doesn't think he's playing his best yet.

The box score

Points: 52

Rebounds: 24

Blocks: 8

Final score: Spurs 112, Mavericks 111

Spurs win streak: 6 straight

What it means

The win is San Antonio's sixth straight, and it came the hard way — a one-point margin decided at the free-throw line in the final half-minute. Nights like this one are why the Spurs' streak feels real rather than lucky: when the game tightens, they have a player who can take over both ends of the floor and close it out.

Behaviour Interactive has now removed the in-game content it gifted to Twitch streamer TheBurntPeanut, and its longer follow-up statement — posted November 3 on the main Dead by Daylight account — admits the studio moved too fast, skipped its own checks, and broke trust with its community.

This is a follow-up to our November 2 post on the first apology. That first statement, posted November 1 on the smaller DbD Pulse account, said the gift was made without appropriate approvals. The new statement, a four-page post, is Behaviour's attempt to answer the questions players were actually asking.

What happened

On October 30, during a Halloween stream, TheBurntPeanut played Dead by Daylight for the first time. Members of Behaviour's team noticed a very large audience in the game's Twitch directory watching a streamer who had not played it before, and saw a chance to welcome new players.

A staff member then gifted TheBurntPeanut access to the game's DLC characters, perks, and in-game currency during the livestream. When TheBurntPeanut offered to give the content back, concerned about the backlash to come, the official account replied in stream chat: "WE CAN TAKE THE HEAT! THEY ARE YOURS TO KEEP!"

The backlash that followed had two layers. Critics raised concerns about Behaviour partnering with TheBurntPeanut, pointing to clips from his past streams, and many players objected to the dismissive tone of the official account's reply. What Behaviour says it got wrong

The November 3 statement breaks the failure into three parts, in the studio's own framing:

Due diligence. The decision to give the creator in-game content and join his chat was made before taking the necessary time to look at whether it was a good idea. It happened quickly and outside the team's usual discussions. Behaviour says it normally looks thoroughly at a new creator's past content to judge whether they represent the values of inclusion its community expects. In this case, it says, that was not taken into proper consideration or discussed as a group.

Internal process. The studio says it did not have stringent enough processes and guardrails to prevent in-game items from being granted directly to players without larger consensus and approval. A team member acted without a supporting system behind them to guide them to another outcome.

Communication standards. The "WE CAN TAKE THE HEAT!" reply, along with some other messages in that session, showed a disregard for the context the team was suddenly in and crossed the line of appropriate responses from the brand, Behaviour says. That reply escalated the situation for the studio and for the community, especially for content creators on the frontlines of those conversations, and that was unacceptable.

Behaviour also states its values in the post: respect, transparency, diversity, and creating a space where people from different backgrounds and experiences feel welcome. Diversity is one of Dead by Daylight's core principles, it says, and when it chooses to work with a creator, it is putting its name next to theirs.

What is changing now

• The content is gone. Behaviour says it has already removed the content that was granted to the creator.

• Creator guidelines are being rewritten. That includes how the team interacts with creators playing Dead by Daylight, who on the team can make those calls, and how to identify creators whose past conduct is in direct opposition to the inclusive community Behaviour says it is trying to build.

• Approval methods are being updated. Request and approval methods for granting content and currency to players are being reworked, with checks and balances at the right time, and the team will be trained on the new processes.

• Creator Program rules are being enforced directly. Creators found to have violated Creator Program policy will result in removal. Any hold or removal will be communicated directly to the creator involved, with no additional communications on social channels or in program Discords. All removals are at the team's discretion.

• Internal action is confidential. Behaviour says it takes the situation very seriously and is taking appropriate action internally, but as a confidential matter it will not share further details.

The statement closes with a direct admission: "This incident has broken trust with our community who hold expectations and regards for the types of people we partner with, but more importantly expectations of the conduct and actions taken by our team in our daily work within this community. Giving a creator a sneak peek at content is not unusual in itself — Behaviour's own first statement notes that. What made this one different, by Behaviour's own account, was speed without approval, a check that never happened, and a public reply that poured fuel on it instead of slowing it down.

The test now is not the statement. It is the next time a big streamer drops into the Fog: who approves the gift, what check happens first, and whether the public response matches the values the studio just restated.

The Elder Scrolls VI Has a Name at Last: Sentinel

By Matt

Eight years after The Elder Scrolls VI was first announced, Bethesda finally gave it a full title — The Elder Scrolls VI: Sentinel — in a video celebrating the studio's 40th anniversary. No release date, no gameplay, just a name — and a desert.

After more than eight years of silence, Bethesda finally said the word. The Elder Scrolls VI is called The Elder Scrolls VI: Sentinel, studio chief Todd Howard revealed on November 3 at the end of a video celebrating Bethesda Game Studios' 40th anniversary. No release date. No platforms. No gameplay. Just a title card, a few seconds of desert, and the loudest single word in gaming in years.

How the reveal actually happened

The announcement came in a video looking back at the studio's history, with Howard turning at the end toward Bethesda's future. The teaser that followed is brief: a camera looking down on the crest of a dune, the Bethesda Game Studios logo flashing as it zooms in, and the wind unearthing an Elder Scroll — complete with an inlaid blue gem and surrounding golden runes. The background cuts to black, the runes hang in the dark, and then the wordmark appears: The Elder Scrolls VI: Sentinel.

Xbox Wire posted the anniversary video and the name reveal on the morning of November 3. Bethesda's own follow-up was deliberately short: development continues, and it will be "some time" before there is more to share.

What the name tells us — and what it doesn't

Sentinel is the capital city of Hammerfell, the desert province on Tamriel's western coast, home of the Redguards. To Hammerfell's northeast is Skyrim; to its southeast is Cyrodiil, where Oblivion takes place. The dune in the teaser fits a province predominantly known for its deserts, and reports ahead of the reveal had already pointed to Hammerfell as the setting.

But be precise about what Bethesda actually confirmed: the title. The studio has not officially announced the game's setting, its story, or its platforms, and the teaser contains no combat, exploration, or gameplay footage of any kind. The Hammerfell reading is the overwhelming consensus of every outlet covering the reveal — and it is a very good reading — but it is still a reading, not a Bethesda announcement.

The teaser does drop one more lore breadcrumb. It closes with a quote attributed to Yazar, a Moth Priest in service to High Queen Iradne of Hammerfell, dated 4E 206: "The scroll offered only a shimmer, a veil thinly lifted to my eyes. A future lost. A victory undone. Sand of time scattered by fate." Skyrim begins in 4E 201, so a quote dated five years later strongly implies Sentinel takes place after Skyrim — not a prequel, and not set between earlier games. Whether the game itself is set in 4E 206 or later is still unclear, and Yazar and Iradne are best read as the first two names attached to the game, not confirmed characters with confirmed roles.

The wait is not over

Here is the cold water, and it matters. Bloomberg's Jason Schreier said on the day of the reveal that the game is still more than two years away. If that reporting holds, Sentinel does not launch before 2028 — a full decade after the game was first announced in June 2018. Bethesda has confirmed no window of its own, so treat 2028 as the earliest date consistent with Schreier's reporting, not as an official date.

That is the strange shape of this reveal: eight years to get a subtitle, and at least two more years to get the game. Fans spent the day celebrating a word, because after eight years, a word is progress. The 40th-anniversary video was a celebration of a studio's past — and, for the first time in years, a real signal about its future. It just isn't a release date, and nobody should pretend it is.

Why this one matters anyway

The Elder Scrolls is one of the few franchises where a title reveal alone moves the whole industry's calendar. Skyrim, released November 11, 2011, is approaching its 15th anniversary, and it has been re-released on essentially everything with a screen since. A new mainline Elder Scrolls game is not just another release — it is the kind of game other publishers schedule around. Sentinel's reveal, even as just a name, finally gives that future a shape: a desert province, a Redguard capital, a prophecy dated five years after Skyrim, and a wait that is measured in years, not months.
